# Writing a Cold CEO Introduction Letter — Method

*A generalized playbook, written from building a real outreach letter (Connective Leadership Institute to KLM's CEO) through roughly a dozen revision rounds. The specific facts belong to that letter; the decisions generalize to any cold introduction letter to a senior, time-poor, skeptical executive.*

---

## 0. The governing constraint

Everything below serves one fact: **the recipient is skeptical, has no time, and is under pressure to show results.** Every structural decision — length, opening, evidence, close — exists to survive a thirty-second read by someone trained to filter out vendor noise. A letter that would work on a patient, curious reader is the wrong letter. Write for the reader who is looking for a reason to stop reading.

---

## 1. The opening: mechanism first, in the reader's own vocabulary

**Decision:** open with what the product *does*, stated as a mechanism, using a metric or concept the reader already manages by — not a rhetorical question, not a scene-setter, not an industry observation.

- Bad pattern (tried and cut): "What if you could track culture the way you track RASK?" — a rhetorical question is a cold-open cliché; a trained reader recognizes the pattern within the first clause and starts skimming.
- Working pattern: "Our product, [Name], turns [the reader's problem] into [a concrete, familiar category of thing], and thereby [the benefit]." State the mechanism as a fact, not a hook.
- **Anchor the new concept to a metric the reader already owns.** A leadership-culture score means nothing to an airline CEO on its own; tied to "the way you already track [metric she manages by]," it inherits credibility from something she already trusts.

---

## 2. Evidence: the hierarchy that actually moves a skeptical reader

Not all proof reads the same to a skeptical executive. In descending order of what a cold reader will credit:

1. **Third-party, checkable validation** — a named award from a real, independent body; a certification; a rating. This is the only category the reader can verify herself in one search, and it is categorically stronger than anything self-reported.
2. **Specific, relevant scale** — client count, headcount trained, years operating, comparable-size or comparable-industry engagements. Credible, but the reader has read this pattern from every vendor; it establishes legitimacy, not proof of results.
3. **Generic scale with no relevance filter** — a raw number with no connection to the reader's industry, size, or situation. Weakest category; reads as vendor boilerplate and can be actively discounted.
4. **A named outcome or result** — what changed for a past client, ideally with a number. This is what a skeptical reader is actually looking for and, in this project, is the one thing that was never available to include. **Its absence was flagged, honestly, in every single revision pass.** A letter can be well-built and still be missing its strongest possible element if the sender doesn't have a real result to cite — that gap should be named, not papered over with more of category 2 or 3.

**Working method:** when the sender lists several possible proof points, sort them into this hierarchy and lead with the highest tier available. In this project, offered options were an industry award (tier 1), annual revenue (not reader-relevant — cut), a certified-practitioner count (tier 2, and useful because it also backs a later claim), and video testimonials (tier 2/4 hybrid, better held for a follow-up than spent in a cold letter).

**Do not include internal financial metrics (the sender's own revenue) as client-facing proof.** It doesn't answer "will this work for me" and can read as an odd, faintly defensive thing to volunteer.

**Hold strong-but-soft evidence (testimonials, references) for the follow-up, not the first letter.** A cold letter that tries to carry every proof point becomes a brochure; save some ammunition for when the reader has already shown interest.

---

## 3. Complicating evidence: state it, don't suppress it

**Decision:** when research turns up a fact that complicates or partially undercuts the pitch's simple version, put it in the letter rather than leave it out.

In this project: the sourced material showed a competitor/sibling-company performance swing was driven mostly by non-management factors (cost and network-mix changes), not leadership. Including that complication — "we won't guess, most of it is X not management, and untangling that mix is exactly what we do" — did two things a cleaner, simpler claim could not:
- It **pre-empted the skeptical reader's own objection** before she could raise it.
- It **converted a limitation into the product's justification** — the very fact that the answer isn't obvious is why the tool is needed.

**Generalizable rule:** a complicating fact, honestly stated and then pivoted into the offer, is more persuasive than a clean claim that a knowledgeable reader can poke a hole in. Never suppress a true complication to keep the pitch simple; use it as the reason the product exists.

---

## 4. Framing risk: organization-level facts, not personal-risk framing

**Decision, reached after an explicit correction mid-project:** a documented organizational fact (e.g., a merger's cultural friction, a known internal tension) belongs in the letter. A pattern framed around what could happen *to the recipient personally* (career risk, job security, "CEOs like you have been removed for this") does not, even when both are equally well-sourced.

- The test that worked: does this fact serve the recipient's curiosity about her own organization, or does it serve the sender's need to create urgency? If the latter, cut or reframe it, however true and well-documented it is.
- A fact stated as **"this predates your tenure"** or **"this is a documented pattern in the group,"** rather than about the recipient's individual performance, keeps analytical value without threat.
- This is a register decision, not an accuracy decision — the underlying research doesn't change; only which findings make it into outbound copy, and how they're framed, changes.

---

## 5. Every capability claim needs a citation or a falsifiable commitment

**Decision:** replace confident adjectives ("real data," "built to answer this") with either (a) a citation to something checkable, or (b) a specific, dated commitment the reader can hold the sender to.

- Weak: "our instruments are built to answer this with real data instead of speculation" — no proof, not falsifiable, indistinguishable from any competitor's copy.
- Strong: "within the first ninety days, we will give you a specific number: [named, measurable output]" — a promise with a date and a shape the reader can check against.
- **When product capability is questioned mid-draft, verify it against what the sender actually has** (a real tool, a real data source) before restating the claim — don't retreat to vaguer language to dodge the question, and don't drop the claim's specificity just because the exact backing wasn't stated up front. Ask; when told the real backing, use it, specifically and by name.

---

## 6. Naming the product and its sub-components — visual hierarchy as information

**Decision:** when a company has one flagship product built from several named components (an assessment suite, a delivery app, an analytics layer), name each component explicitly and apply one consistent visual treatment to the flagship name (in this case: colored, bold, italic) and a second, lighter treatment (bold only) to sub-components.

- This lets a skimming reader distinguish "the thing I'm being sold" from "the parts that make it work" without reading closely.
- **Keep the treatment mechanically consistent everywhere the name appears** — every instance, not just the first. Inconsistent formatting of a repeated proper noun reads as sloppy in exactly the way a grammar error does.
- If several named components appear in one sentence, that sentence is probably overloaded (see §8) — consider whether the components need to be split across sentences so each is legible on its own.

---

## 7. The close: capability and terms, not a meeting request

**Decision:** end with what the sender can do and by when, not a request for the reader's time.

- Weak: "I would value twenty minutes to discuss..." — asks the busy reader to do the work of scheduling, and signals the letter itself wasn't enough to make the case.
- Strong: "We can start by [date]. There is no new staffing required. We deliver [specific named artifact] within [specific window]." — states terms; the reader's next action, if she takes one, is obvious and low-friction (reply, forward, or ignore) rather than "find twenty minutes."
- Naming a concrete date (not "soon" or "immediately") makes the commitment falsifiable, which matters more than it sounds like it should — see §5.

---

## 8. Editing discipline: what to check on every pass, in order

Applied as a repeatable sequence across many revision rounds:

1. **Read it cold, as the specific recipient** — busy, skeptical, under pressure, looking for a reason to stop reading. Not as the sender wants it read.
2. **Check every sentence for a verb and a subject.** Long revision chains, especially when sentences get spliced or reordered, produce fragments ("With a baseline assessment..." with no main clause) more often than expected. Check this explicitly every time text is restructured, not just on first draft.
3. **Check subject-verb agreement on the product name specifically** — a named product ("The Future") is grammatically singular even when the sentence is long and the verb is far from the subject; this is where agreement errors cluster.
4. **Check for repeated words in one sentence** ("documented history... is well documented") — a common artifact of iterative editing, where an earlier fix leaves a duplicate behind a few words later.
5. **Check punctuation consistency** (one dash style throughout, not a mix of em dash and spaced en dash) — a mechanical fix, not a content one, and one the writer should make without being asked.
6. **Check that any listed items are the same *kind* of thing** — a list mixing concrete events ("a stalled negotiation, a hire who doesn't land") with abstract feelings ("alienated teams") reads unevenly; a skeptical reader notices the softest item in a list.
7. **Check density per paragraph** — count how many distinct claims one paragraph is carrying (a fact, a nuance, a commitment, three product names). If a reader would need to reread it to track the claims, split it.
8. **Check for the one metaphor or slogan sitting inside an otherwise literal passage** — a single "North Star" or similar phrase stands out precisely because everything around it is numbers and named deliverables; cut it or replace it with something equally concrete.
9. **Enforce the length/format constraint last, after content is right** — cutting for a page limit before the substance is settled tends to cut the wrong material.
10. **State remaining gaps honestly rather than papering over them** — if the strongest possible evidence (a named outcome, not just scale) isn't available, say so plainly in review rather than pretend the letter is complete. Fix it the moment better material becomes available, and don't let sentence-level polishing substitute for a missing proof point.

---

## 9. On the person who is best positioned to supply what's missing

The single biggest lever on this letter's credibility, across every revision, was not a sentence-level edit — it was the sender producing real, specific evidence (an award, a certified-practitioner count) that no amount of rewriting could substitute for. **The generalizable point: once the prose is clean and the structure is sound, further improvement depends on the sender supplying better raw material, not on more editing.** Recognize when a letter has hit that ceiling and say so, rather than continuing to polish sentences in place of asking for the fact that would actually move the letter forward.

---

*This method is recipient-agnostic by design. Swap the audience (any senior, skeptical, time-poor decision-maker), the product, and the evidence — keep the evidence hierarchy (§2), the complicating-fact discipline (§3), the organization-vs-personal framing rule (§4), the falsifiable-claim standard (§5), and the editing checklist (§8) unchanged.*
