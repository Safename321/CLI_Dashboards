## MARJAN RINTEL — President & CEO, KLM

### Source 1 — Aviation Week / Routes (David Casey), 17 December 2025
**URL:** https://aviationweek.com/air-transport/airports-networks/klm-targets-selective-growth-three-new-european-routes
**Setting:** Interview at the CAPA Airline Leader Summit World, Lisbon, 12 December 2025. **Fetched and read in full.**

**Direct quotes (verbatim):**
- On growth constraints: *"The main thing in the Netherlands is [it is] impossible to grow. There's no growth anymore. So, you need to make that sense."*
- On the hub model: *"It's important for us to feed our destinations, and that's the reason why, fundamentally, we still have [a] hub-and-spoke [model]."* She pointed to the importance of *"unique, smaller destinations"* that support the hub.
- On policy constraints: *"Reduction is not the goal in itself. You need a strong economy, you need strong infrastructure and you need a home carrier for your economy, for businesses in the Netherlands and for all the connections we have."*

**Reported facts (journalist, not Rintel):**
- Government-imposed cap at Schiphol of **478,000 annual flight movements, down from 500,000**, in force from the start of the IATA winter season, **26 October 2025**. Subject to ongoing legal challenge by the industry.
- Netherlands moving toward higher aviation taxes; under the latest budget proposal, flight taxes would rise again **from 2027**, potentially reaching **~€30 per short-haul ticket and more than €70 for long-haul**.
- New summer 2026 routes: Jersey, Santiago de Compostela, Oviedo — all KLM Cityhopper, Embraer 175/190.

**What this supports:** a sourced concern about growth being structurally capped at the home hub, and the strategic response being selective/quality network growth rather than expansion.

**What this supports:** a sourced concern about fragile profitability — explicitly *"one good half-year does not make KLM structurally strong and robust"* — plus cost pressure and productivity as the stated focus.

# CROSS-CUTTING FINDING — the multi-brand "friction" premise is not sourced

The existing report attributes culture-clash friction to the three European group CEOs:
- **CEO #6 / Spohr:** "five airlines with clashing national cultures"
- **CEO #7 / Gallego:** "four carriers pulling in different directions"
- **CEO #8 / Smith:** "French-Dutch cultural leadership friction producing coordination drag"

**All three of these executives, in sourced interviews, describe multi-brand structure as deliberate and advantageous, not as friction:**
- **Smith** (Airline Observer, 7 Jul 2025): Air France and KLM are *"independently run companies"* focused on *"different customer segments."*
- **Gallego** (Brunswick Review, Jan 2026): IAG's model *"emphasizes the operational independence of the individual airlines."*
- **Spohr** (FlightGlobal, May 2026): credits the **multi-hub system** for *limiting* strike damage.

**Implication:** the "clashing cultures" framing is CLI's analytical inference, not something these CEOs said. It may still be a valid diagnosis — but it must be presented as CLI's read of a structural condition, **not as a concern the CEO voiced.** Presenting it as their stated concern is not supportable and would not survive a challenge from a well-informed reader.

**What IS sourced and usable for the same argument:** multi-brand groups deliberately run their airlines as independent operating cultures (Smith, Gallego, both explicitly) — and one of them, Lufthansa, is spending **€190m in two quarters** on labour stoppages (€40m Q1 + €150m Q2, FlightGlobal). The premise that these groups contain genuinely different behavioural systems is well sourced; the claim that their CEOs complain about it is not.

# ROUND 8 — THE DEPARTURE WAVE: cause-of-exit traced to source (14 August 2026)

**The finding for CLI:** Of the ~20 major airline CEOs profiled, at least 7 exited within roughly 15 months (Dec 2025 – Q3 2026). In nearly every case, the airline's *official* reason ("retirement," "personal reasons," "long-planned succession") diverges from the *traceable* trigger — a communication failure, a cultural/political misjudgment, an operational-culture breakdown, or a safety/compliance-culture lapse. **The gap between the stated reason and the real cause is precisely the behavioral signal CLI's instruments are built to measure — and it is the reason a sitting CEO cannot see their own exposure until it is too late.**

## 1. Michael Rousseau — Air Canada (leaving by end Q3 2026)
- **Official reason:** "retirement… after nearly two decades," board says succession planning "underway for more than two years" (Air Canada press release, 30-Mar-26).
- **Traceable trigger:** Delivered English-only video condolences after the fatal LaGuardia Air Canada Express crash (23-Mar-26) in officially-bilingual Canada. PM Mark Carney said it showed "a lack of compassion and judgment"; Quebec premier and lawmakers called for his resignation; he was summoned to Ottawa. Retirement announced **days later**. Also cited: prior mishandling of a **four-day flight-attendant strike** and years of criticism over his poor French.
- **CLI read:** a *communication and cultural-judgment* failure, not a strategy or numbers failure — Air Canada was financially sound. The board's "long-planned" framing is the cover; the successor criteria now explicitly require French fluency, revealing the true cause. Sources: Fox Business, Global News, PBS/AP, aerotime (all 30-Mar-26).

## 2. Pieter Elbers — IndiGo (resigned 10-Mar-26, effective immediately) — FORMER KLM CEO
- **Official reason:** "personal reasons," notice period waived (resignation letter, 10-Mar-26).
- **Traceable trigger:** IndiGo's **worst operational meltdown in history** — ~4,500 flights cancelled in December 2025, ~980,000 passengers affected, when the airline was unprepared for new DGCA crew-fatigue rules. DGCA issued Elbers a personal 24-hour show-cause notice (7-Dec-25) and a record ₹22.2 crore fine. Investigations found **"over-optimisation"** — schedules run so tight there was no buffer — plus weak crew-planning software and rapid expansion without operational backbone. Elbers "was heavily blamed… had to do quite the apology tour."
- **CLI read:** an *operational-culture and leadership-pipeline* failure — growth prioritized over resilience, no buffer when behavior/process was stressed. "Personal reasons" is the euphemism; observers were "few convinced." **Directly relevant to Rintel: Elbers is a former KLM CEO — her own predecessor lineage — and the failure was cultural/operational, not commercial.** Sources: Skift, OMAAT, BBC, Reuters, Open Magazine (Mar-26).

## 3. Campbell Wilson — Air India (resigned 7-Apr-26)
- **Official reason:** stepping down for the company's "next growth phase"; airline says he'd flagged intent in 2024.
- **Traceable trigger:** The **Ahmedabad AI171 Boeing 787 crash (12-Jun-25, 260 dead)** — deadliest aviation disaster of the 2020s — followed by regulators citing Air India for safety-culture failures (flying an aircraft **eight times without an airworthiness certificate**, operating planes without checking emergency equipment). In December the airline itself acknowledged a **"need for urgent improvements in process discipline, communication, and compliance culture."** Combined loss ₹98bn ($1.05bn) FY24-25.
- **CLI read:** a *safety- and compliance-culture* failure, self-admitted by the airline in cultural language. The "next growth phase" framing masks a crash-and-scrutiny exit. Sources: Independent, AirInsight, aerotime, Business Chief (Apr-26).

## 4. Bilal Ekşi — Turkish Airlines (retired, announced 9-Apr-26)

## THE CROSS-CUTTING PATTERN (the centerpiece argument)
Across the seven, three exits (Rousseau, Elbers, Wilson) trace to **behavioral/culture failures** — communication, judgment, operational-culture, compliance-culture — that the airlines themselves described in cultural language ("compassion and judgment," "communication and compliance culture," "over-optimisation"). That blind spot is what CLI measures. # ROUND 9 — Air France / KLM cultural divergence and the performance gap (16 August 2026)

## The documented culture gap
- **2017 internal works-council report** (Dutch/French researchers, 50 senior managers interviewed): "the Dutch and the French do not understand each other, both want to be the dominant partner and do not put the group's interests first." AF staff felt KLM prioritized its own interests; KLM staff felt AF didn't respect theirs. (DutchNews.nl, FlyerTalk translation of NOS report.)
- **Decision-making style clash**, from interviewed employees (Gestion des Risques Interculturels): Dutch staff describe French hierarchy as opaque ("everything is arranged behind the scenes"); French staff describe Dutch pre-meetings before the real meeting as making formal meetings feel like rubber-stamping. "The boss is the enemy, whether it's a good boss or a bad boss" (Dutch employee, on hierarchical distance).
- **Governance friction persists at the state level**: French and Dutch governments have publicly clashed over influence in the Group; a French official accused the Netherlands of acting "like a corporate raider" (2019, Gulf News). Dutch government said it had "too little leverage in KLM" to protect Schiphol's status as a hub.
- **Historically KLM was the stronger performer** despite the friction — 2018: KLM profitable (+€60m) while Air France lost €178m amid strikes; Dutch unions saw "no way out of marriage" precisely *because* KLM kept outperforming while carrying Air France.

## The reversal — current data cuts against a simple "culture explains it" story
- **AirInsight (19-Feb-26), on FY2025 results:** *"It's not that long ago that when speaking of Air France-KLM's results, the onus was on Air France. That's no more. It's Dutch flag carrier KLM that has work to do."* Air France 6.7% margin vs. KLM 3.2%. KLM hit by a 41% Schiphol tariff increase (~€100m extra landing fees, ~€150m revenue impact) and lower-yielding connecting traffic, especially Africa/Asia.
- **Avianticslabs (H1 2026), a sharper and more honest finding:** by H1 2026 KLM's margin (4.5%) had actually overtaken Air France's (4.1%) — but the piece is explicit that **management is only one of three causes, and not the main one**: *"Three things are doing the work, and only one of them is management."* The other two: (1) **cargo** — KLM cargo traffic +23.8% vs. AF +1.1%, largely because Schiphol geography/freighter positioning captured a freight demand windfall; (2) **capacity mix** — KLM grew passenger capacity +6.0% while AF *shrank* -1.2% as short-haul Orly flying moved to Transavia France, a structural network shift, not a performance story.

**Interpretive note for CLI, stated carefully:** the raw margin swing between AF and KLM in 2025–26 is **not** cleanly attributable to management or culture — a credible outlet attributes most of the swing to Schiphol cargo geography and a network capacity reallocation. This matters for how any CLI pitch uses the AF/KLM performance gap: the *documented, sourced* culture friction (works-council report, decision-making style clash) is real and longstanding, but should be presented as its own finding — not conflated with the current margin gap, which has a large non-culture explanation. Overstating the causal link would be exactly the kind of unsupported assertion the earlier report was criticized for.

## What's usable and defensible
1. **The 2017 works-council report and the ongoing French/Dutch governance friction are real, sourced, longstanding evidence of a management-culture divide** within the Group — independent of any given year's financials.
2. **The margin gap between AF and KLM is real but multi-causal** — Schiphol tariffs/geography, connecting-traffic mix, and cargo positioning explain most of the recent swing; management (Back on Track) is a real but partial contributor, per Rintel's own claimed "€450m exceeded target."
3. The honest CLI pitch is therefore: *not* "KLM's margin gap proves a culture problem," but "a documented, decades-long management-culture divide exists in this group, alongside operating pressures — and CLI measures the behavioral dimension that financial reporting can't isolate from the operational one."
