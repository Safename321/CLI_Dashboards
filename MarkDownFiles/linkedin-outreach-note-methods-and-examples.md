# Communication-Style Methods + Example Notes

A reference for writing short, high-altitude outreach that reads as a peer, not a vendor. Distilled from working notes; the Joe Creed note is the worked example.

---

## Communication-style methods (the transferable, unique rules)

1. **Mirror the reader's own words in the first clause.** Recognition, not flattery. (Creed: "more than a job," his exact phrase.) Quote-and-turn beats "great post."
2. **Set a personal stake against theirs — peer, not pitch.** Their devotion beside yours ("my life's work"), so it reads founder-to-founder, never vendor-to-buyer.
3. **Give one legible, non-transactional reason for the touch.** Their event / challenge / post — so you're contributing, not asking. (Creed: "I registered for *your* Building the Future Workforce.")
4. **Aim the emotional appeal at the reader's real motivation.** For a lifer/steward: legacy — "leave it better for those who come next." Match the motive to the person.
5. **Close on ONE payload in the reader's own language.** Tune the last line to *this* reader: financial/KPI for a CPA ("behavior mods → performance KPIs & EPS"), integration for an acquirer, autonomy's human layer for a strategist. One payload, not three.
6. **Cut the generic sign-off.** Drop "would value comparing notes as you…" filler when space is tight. Every clause earns its characters.
7. **Keep the org soft in cold/public contexts; name it once in a warm DM.** In cold notes and public comments the headline + link are the funnel — don't pitch by name. A first-degree DM can name it once, framed as an idea.
8. **One embedded link, max — framed as sharing a story, not selling.** And only where a URL won't read as spam. Omit URLs from cold connection *invites* (they can dampen acceptance); fine in a message after connect.
9. **Match the reader's sophistication and prose; never youthen the voice.** British spelling for British readers, economist's vocabulary for financiers, strategist's frame ("where to play / how to win") for strategists.
10. **Name the objection to your own claim once, then turn it** (Goodhart/Campbell). Pre-empting the "once a measure becomes a target it distorts" critique separates you from every vendor in the feed. Use once, well.
11. **Never ask for what isn't certain to be granted — state the value instead.** A cold note that asks for a meeting, a call, or "twenty minutes" invites a no: the reader hasn't yet decided you're worth the time, so requesting it is asking a stranger for a favor. Replace the ask with the reader's payoff, asserted in the second person — not "I'd value comparing notes," but **"You'll value seeing [the specific thing they get]."** The touch *presents an opportunity*; it never *requests* one. Reserve the ask for a warm reply, where a yes is already likely. (Corollary: strike "I'd value…," "I'd welcome…," "would love to…," "I'd value your read/time" from all cold copy — every one of them hands the reader an easy decline.)

## Worked example — a good short note (LinkedIn, =300 characters)

**Target:** Joe Creed, Chairman & CEO, Caterpillar Inc. (CPA; ~30 years at the company; stewardship/workforce-driven)

> Joe - after nearly 30 years Caterpillar is more than a job to you; I feel that about my life's work: connectiveleadership.com/#about. I registered for Building the Future Workforce to leave it better for those who come next - and to see how behavior mods eventually pay off in performance KPIs & EPS.

**Which methods it uses:** 1 (mirrors "more than a job"), 2 ("my life's work"), 3 (registered for his challenge), 4 ("leave it better for those who come next"), 5 (CPA payload: behavior mods -> KPIs & EPS), 6 (no filler close), 8 (one link, framed as story).

## Worked example — the way we comment (LinkedIn, under an executive's post)

**Target:** Brent Saunders (Chairman & CEO, Bausch + Lomb) — post: "Buying an #AI tool is easy. Building an organization that knows how to use it well is harder." Thread context: full of vendors pitching their products.

> "Buying an AI tool is easy. Building an organization that knows how to use it well is harder." There's a third layer, maybe the decisive one: knowing what the tool was built from. AI in general is remarkable — adaptive, context-reading, logical, with a breadth of knowledge no human library matches. But engines are like cars, tools, and boats: each is only as good as what it was engineered for. And because AI is now easy to manufacture, it's being produced the way all easy things are — some with purpose, much of it carelessly, and most of it as an answer in search of a question rather than a means to an end.
>
> The difference shows up in what sits underneath. We spent years accumulating 45,000+ observations of workplace behavior across widely varying environments before we let an AI agent train on any of it. That order of operations matters: science first, machine second. Grounded in evidence, AI genuinely advances what the research can see. Without it, AI remains a brilliant pattern-spotter — and patterns without a science behind them come in exactly two kinds: the meaningless, and the not-yet-understood. Organizations that can tell the difference are the ones that turn the tool into lasting advantage.

**The comment formula this example sets:**

1. **Open on the author's exact words in quotes**, then add a layer they didn't — never restate, never praise ("great post" is banned).
2. **Concede AI's (or the subject's) genuine strengths first** — adaptive, context-reading, broad — so the critique that follows reads as balance, not contrarianism.
3. **Carry the argument on one plain analogy** (engines: cars, tools, boats). Everyday objects, no jargon, no metaphors that need decoding.
4. **Name the market failure without naming anyone**: "an answer in search of a question rather than a means to an end" — in a vendor-filled thread, the contrast does its own work.
5. **Deliver the credential as practitioner testimony, first person**: "We spent years accumulating 45,000+ observations... before we let an AI agent train on any of it." Never the company name in the comment — the profile headline is the funnel; "we" + experience reads as authority, a name-drop reads as a pitch.
6. **State the doctrine in four words**: science first, machine second.
7. **Close on a liftable line** — "patterns without a science behind them come in exactly two kinds: the meaningless, and the not-yet-understood." One line someone could quote or repost is worth the whole comment.
8. **Two paragraphs, standalone comment** — never a reply under another vendor's comment; scale length to the room (data-serious rooms tolerate ~180 words; launch posts want less).

**When a second paragraph on teamwork/measurement is wanted** (the Canney/Capozzi pattern): tag the people the original post tagged, quote each person quoted, hedge borrowed quotations ("the line often credited to Aristotle"), and end by handing the author's own phrase back to them.

## Worked example — the LinkedIn connection InMail (2 paragraphs, cold, no mutuals)

**Target:** Ola Källenius, CEO of Mercedes-Benz (Swede leading a German icon; formed in Alabama; global plant ramp-ups; told apprentices the key qualities are "curiosity, flexibility and a winning attitude").

**Subject:** The quiet variable under the biggest launch programme in your history

> Ola — a Swede leading Germany's flagship company, shaped professionally in Alabama, now ramping plants from Kecskemét to Beijing: you've lived the thesis I've spent my career proving. How people work together is cultural, learnable, and decisive — and it can be measured. You recently told your apprentices that the qualities that matter most are "curiosity, flexibility and a winning attitude." I'd add just one thing: all three are behaviors. And behaviors can be measured — which means they can be found, developed, and multiplied across 100,000 people, rather than hoped for.
>
> That's the work of the Connective Leadership Institute, which I lead: forty years of research, behavioral data from participants in 133 countries in 19 languages, measuring how organizations actually execute, decide, and integrate — carried through to financial outcomes a board can act on. As Mercedes runs the biggest launch programme in its history across a production network spanning Alabama, Hungary, and China, that human layer is the quiet variable under every ramp-up — the difference between a trained plant and an effective one. I'd value comparing notes: one plant, one launch, one number. The line often credited to Aristotle says it best: "We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act, but a habit." Measuring how people work together is how the habit stays honest — and excellence as a habit is the oldest Mercedes idea there is: the best or nothing. connectiveleadership.com/#about

**The InMail formula this example sets:**

1. **Subject line in the reader's own recurring phrase** ("biggest launch programme in your history") — the message reads as written for them, not merged from a template.
2. **Mirror their biography when there is no post to quote** — a cold InMail's strongest mirror is the reader's own life stated as your thesis ("a Swede... shaped in Alabama... ramping Kecskemét to Beijing").
3. **Quote something they actually said** (the apprentice line) — the single highest-credibility signal available to a stranger; proves you read them.
4. **Turn the quote once, gently**: "all three are behaviors — and behaviors can be measured."
5. **Name the institute ONCE, in a private message only** (public comments never name it; DMs/InMails may — the channel changes the rule).
6. **Credential in one breath**: 40 years, 133 countries, 19 languages, carried to financial outcomes. Numbers, not adjectives.
7. **Business trigger in their language** — tie the thesis to the thing they are publicly doing right now (ramp-ups, launches, integrations).
8. **Bounded ask, six words**: "one plant, one launch, one number" — concrete enough to forward or decline; never an open-ended request for time.
9. **Close: hedged quotation -> turn onto THEIR heritage -> URL last** ("the best or nothing" -> connectiveleadership.com/#about). The link comes after the idea has earned the click, framed as where the story lives, not a call to action.
10. **Never knock twice.** No follow-up on silence; a comment on a later post is the only acceptable second surface.

## Worked example — the value-forward connection note (no ask)

**Target:** Raj Subramaniam, CEO, FedEx (engineer by training; leads ~500,000 people; FedEx's founding creed is *People-Service-Profit*, with People first).

> Raj — FedEx was built on People-Service-Profit, and People comes first. Our app makes it measurable: consolidated workforce feedback from your 500,000 people, straight to you — not filtered up six management layers. You'll value seeing the organization's overall, distilled and examined.

**Which methods it uses:** 1 (mirrors his own creed, "People comes first"), 5 (one payload in his language: consolidated workforce feedback delivered to the CEO), 6 (no filler close), 8 (no link on a cold invite), and above all **11 — no ask.** The note never requests a meeting or his time; it closes on *his* payoff ("You'll value seeing…"), asserting the value of the opportunity rather than begging entry to it.

**The generalized template (rule 11 in practice):**

> [First name] — [their company/creed, mirrored in their own words], and [the value that creed puts first]. Our [product] makes it measurable: [one concrete payload delivered straight to them], not [the friction or filter it removes]. You'll value seeing [the specific outcome they get].

Swap the creed, the payload, and the outcome; keep the shape. The last line is always *the reader's benefit stated as fact*, never a request. If you catch yourself writing "I'd value," "I'd welcome," or "twenty minutes," delete it and finish the sentence from the reader's side of the table.
